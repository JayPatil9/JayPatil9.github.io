This post is a prequel to my next post, where we will be solving problems which are notorious and may give you headache if you were to implement it with brute force. Just a brief idea about what type of problems we are going to solve: Finding a subset of numbers from a given set such that the XOR of all the numbers in that subset is maximized.
But before we do some magic, we need to talk about **Linear Algebra**.

Don't panic. We aren't going to calculate eigenvalues by hand or invert massive matrices. We are just going to steal the absolute best concepts from that class you might have dozed off in.

### 1. What is a Vector?

This is pretty simple, right?
In layman's terms, a vector is an arrow with "magnitude and direction."

For our purposes, we need to look at vectors as **lists of coordinates**.
Take `V = [2, 5, 1]`. That isn't just a list with three integers; it represents a single, exact location in a 3D space. You go 2 steps along the x-axis, 5 steps along the y-axis, and 1 step up the z-axis.

### 2. Linear Combinations

Imagine you have two vectors in a 2D space: `V1 = [1, 0]` and `V2 = [0, 1]`.

By multiplying these vectors by some numbers (scalars) and adding them together, you can literally create *any* 2D coordinate in the universe.
Want to reach the coordinate `[7, -3]`? Easy:
`(7 * V1) + (-3 * V2) = [7, -3]`

This mixing and matching of ingredients is called a **Linear Combination**. The set of all possible coordinates you can reach by mixing your ingredients is called your **Span**. With just those two simple vectors, your span is the entire infinite 2D plane.

### 3. The Redundancy Problem

In programming, we hate redundancy. It wastes memory and CPU cycles. Linear Algebra hates redundancy too.

Let's say I give you three vectors:

* `V1 = [1, 0]`
* `V2 = [0, 1]`
* `V3 = [2, 3]`

Can `V3` help you reach any new coordinates that you couldn't already reach with just `V1` and `V2`?

**Nope.** Because `V3` is literally just `(2 * V1) + (3 * V2)`. It brings absolutely zero new information to the table. In math terms, `V3` is **linearly dependent**. Even if we discard `V3`, our "Span" doesn't shrink at all; we can still reach every point in the 2D plane.

### 4. The Basis

A **Basis** of a vector space is a tightly packed set of vectors that are:

1. **Linearly Independent**
2. **Spanning:** They can be combined to build *everything* in that space.

Think of a Basis as the ultimate **Key** for a mathematical dimension. Instead of carrying around infinite points to describe a 3D space, you just need a basis of exactly 3 vectors. From those 3 core vectors, you can perfectly reconstruct the entire 3D space.

### The Big Picture: Why Are We Talking About This?

Right now, you are probably wondering what Linear Algebra has to do with binary numbers and the bitwise XOR operator (`^`).

Here is the spoiler: **Consider binary numbers as vectors.**

Think about it. What is the number 13? In binary, it’s `1101`. That looks an awful lot like a 4-dimensional vector `[1, 1, 0, 1]`, doesn't it?

And If you take two binary vectors and add them component by component, but treat every sum the way binary arithmetic does, you get XOR. The rule is simple: `0 + 0 = 0`, `0 + 1 = 1`, `1 + 0 = 1`, and `1 + 1 = 0` because the carry disappears. That is exactly what XOR does, bit by bit.

But standard math has a problem: when you add `1 + 1`, you get `2`. But in binary, there are only two symbols: `0` and `1`. So, experts invented a strange alternate space, a finite field, where numbers wrap around, and `1 + 1` simply cancels out to `0`.

So when we say XOR is vector addition in this alternate universe, we mean each bit is added independently, and the result stays inside the set `{0, 1}`. In other words, XOR is just ordinary addition over a field where `1 + 1` wraps back to `0` instead of becoming `2`.

If XOR is just vector addition in an alternate universe, that means every single rule of Linear Algebra applies to it. And if the rules apply, it means we can find the **Basis** of a massive list of numbers. We can compress $10^5$ integers down to a microscopic key of just 20 numbers, without losing any information about their potential XOR combinations.
